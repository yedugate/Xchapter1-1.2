2. Meaning of Divisibility

Let \(a\) and \(b\) be integers, and \(a \neq 0\).

We say that:

\[a \mid b\]

if there exists an integer \(k\) such that:

\[b = ak\]

This is read as:

“a divides b.”

Example:

\[4 \mid 28\]

because:

\[28 = 4 \times 7\]

Here, 7 is an integer.

So 4 divides 28.

But:

\[4 \nmid 30\]

because:

\[30 \div 4 = 7.5\]

7.5 is not an integer.

So 4 does not divide 30.

3. Simple Meaning for Average Students

A number is divisible by another number if division leaves remainder 0.

Example:

\[36 \div 6 = 6\]

Remainder is 0.

So:

\[6 \mid 36\]

But:

\[38 \div 6 = 6 \text{ remainder } 2\]

Remainder is not 0.

So:

\[6 \nmid 38\]

5. Divisor Cannot Be Zero

In:

\[a \mid b\]

the divisor \(a\) cannot be zero.

Why?

Because division by zero is not defined.

Example:

\[0 \mid 5\]

would mean:

\[5 = 0 \times k\]

But:

\[0 \times k = 0\]

for every integer \(k\).

So it can never become 5.

Therefore:

\[0 \nmid 5\]

But:

\[5 \mid 0\]

is true because:

\[0 = 5 \times 0\]

So remember:

\[0 \nmid a \quad \text{for } a \neq 0\]

but:

\[a \mid 0 \quad \text{for } a \neq 0\]

7. Factor and Multiple

If:

\[a \mid b\]

then:

  • \(a\) is a factor of \(b\)
  • \(a\) is a divisor of \(b\)
  • \(b\) is a multiple of \(a\)

Example:

\[6 \mid 42\]

because:

\[42 = 6 \times 7\]

So:

  • 6 is a factor of 42
  • 6 is a divisor of 42
  • 42 is a multiple of 6

8. Important Properties of Divisibility

Property 1: Every non-zero integer divides itself

\[a \mid a\]

because:

\[a = a \times 1\]

Example:

\[9 \mid 9\]

Property 2: 1 divides every integer

\[1 \mid a\]

because:

\[a = 1 \times a\]

Example:

\[1 \mid 100\]

Property 3: Every non-zero integer divides zero

\[a \mid 0\]

because:

\[0 = a \times 0\]

Example:

\[12 \mid 0\]

Property 4: Zero divides no non-zero integer

\[0 \nmid a\]

where:

\[a \neq 0\]

Example:

\[0 \nmid 25\]

Property 5: Transitive Property

If:

\[a \mid b\]

and:

\[b \mid c\]

then:

\[a \mid c\]

Proof

Since:

\[a \mid b\]

we can write:

\[b = ax\]

for some integer \(x\).

Since:

\[b \mid c\]

we can write:

\[c = by\]

for some integer \(y\).

Substitute \(b = ax\):

\[c = (ax)y\]
\[c = a(xy)\]

Since (xy) is an integer:

\[a \mid c\]

Example

\[3 \mid 12\]

and:

\[12 \mid 60\]

Therefore:

\[3 \mid 60\]

Property 6: Linear Combination Property

If:

\[a \mid b\]

and:

\[a \mid c\]

then:

\[a \mid (b+c)\]
\[a \mid (b-c)\]

and more generally:

\[a \mid (mb+nc)\]

where \(m,n \in \mathbb{Z}\).

Proof

Since:

\[a \mid b\]
\[b = ax\]

Since:

\[a \mid c\]
\[c = ay\]

Now:

\[mb+nc = m(ax)+n(ay)\]
\[mb+nc = a(mx+ny)\]

Since \(mx+ny\) is an integer:

\[a \mid (mb+nc)\]

Example

Given:

\[5 \mid 20\]

and:

\[5 \mid 35\]

Then:

\[5 \mid (20+35)\]
\[5 \mid 55\]

Also:

\[5 \mid (35-20)\]
\[5 \mid 15\]

And:

\[5 \mid (2 \times 20 + 3 \times 35)\]
\[5 \mid 145\]

This is a very important topper-level property.

9. Euclid’s Division Lemma

Euclid’s Division Lemma says:

For any two positive integers \(a\) and \(b\), there exist unique integers \(q\) and \(r\) such that:

\[a = bq + r\]

where:

\[0 \leq r < b\]

Here:

Symbol Meaning
\(a\) dividend
\(b\) divisor
\(q\) quotient
\(r\) remainder

Example:

Divide 43 by 6.

\[43 = 6 \times 7 + 1\]

Here:

\[a=43,\quad b=6,\quad q=7,\quad r=1\]

Since remainder is 1:

\[6 \nmid 43\]

But:

\[42 = 6 \times 7 + 0\]

Since remainder is 0:

\[6 \mid 42\]

10. Euclid’s Division Algorithm

Euclid’s Division Algorithm is used to find the HCF of two positive integers.

Method

To find HCF of \(a\) and \(b\), where \(a>b\):

Step 1:

\[a = bq + r\]

Step 2: If \(r=0\), then \(b\) is the HCF.

Step 3: If \(r \neq 0\), divide \(b\) by \(r\).

Step 4: Continue until remainder becomes 0.

The divisor at the final step is the HCF.

Example 1: Find HCF of 135 and 225

Since 225 > 135:

\[225 = 135 \times 1 + 90\]

Now:

\[135 = 90 \times 1 + 45\]

Now:

\[90 = 45 \times 2 + 0\]

Therefore:

\[\text{HCF}(225,135)=45\]

Example 2: Find HCF of 252 and 198

\[252 = 198 \times 1 + 54\]
\[198 = 54 \times 3 + 36\]
\[54 = 36 \times 1 + 18\]
\[36 = 18 \times 2 + 0\]

Therefore:

\[\text{HCF}(252,198)=18\]
CBSE Class 10 • Real Numbers

11. Why Euclid’s Algorithm Works

This is a topper-level understanding.

If:

\[a = bq + r\]

then:

\[r = a - bq\]

Any common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\) must also divide:

\[a - bq\]

So it divides \(r\).

Therefore:

\[\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r)\]

This is the main logic behind Euclid’s Algorithm.

12. Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Every composite number can be expressed as a product of primes in a unique way, except for the order of the prime factors.

Example:

\[180 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5\]

This prime factorisation is unique.

You may write:

\[180 = 3^2 \times 2^2 \times 5\]

but the prime factors and powers remain the same.

13. Prime Factorisation Method

Example

Find prime factorisation of 360.

\[360 = 36 \times 10\]
\[360 = (2^2 \times 3^2)(2 \times 5)\]
\[360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5\]

So:

\[360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5\]

14. Checking Divisibility Using Prime Factorisation

To check whether \(a \mid b\), compare the prime factors of \(a\) and \(b\).

If every prime factor of \(a\) occurs in \(b\) with equal or greater power, then:

\[a \mid b\]

Example 1

Check whether:

\[18 \mid 540\]

Prime factorise:

\[18 = 2 \times 3^2\]
\[540 = 54 \times 10\]
\[540 = (2 \times 3^3)(2 \times 5)\]
\[540 = 2^2 \times 3^3 \times 5\]

Now compare:

\[18 = 2^1 \times 3^2\]
\[540 = 2^2 \times 3^3 \times 5\]

540 contains at least \(2^1\) and \(3^2\).

Therefore:

\[18 \mid 540\]

Example 2

Check whether:

\[72 \mid 2160\]
\[72 = 2^3 \times 3^2\]
\[2160 = 2^4 \times 3^3 \times 5\]

Since 2160 contains at least \(2^3\) and \(3^2\):

\[72 \mid 2160\]

15. HCF by Prime Factorisation

To find HCF:

Take the lowest power of common prime factors.

Example:

Find HCF of 144 and 360.

\[144 = 2^4 \times 3^2\]
\[360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5\]

Common primes are 2 and 3.

Take lowest powers:

\[2^3 \times 3^2\]
\[= 8 \times 9\]
\[= 72\]

So:

\[\text{HCF}(144,360)=72\]
CBSE Class 10 • Real Numbers

16. LCM by Prime Factorisation

To find LCM:

Take the highest power of all prime factors.

Example:

Find LCM of 144 and 360.

\[144 = 2^4 \times 3^2\]
\[360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5\]

Highest powers:

\[2^4 \times 3^2 \times 5\]
\[= 16 \times 9 \times 5\]
\[= 720\]

So:

\[\text{LCM}(144,360)=720\]
CBSE Class 10 • Real Numbers

17. Relation Between HCF and LCM

For two positive integers \(a\) and \(b\):

\[a \times b = \text{HCF}(a,b) \times \text{LCM}(a,b)\]

Example:

Let:

\[a = 144,\quad b = 360\]
\[\text{HCF}=72\]
\[\text{LCM}=720\]

Now:

\[144 \times 360 = 51840\]

and:

\[72 \times 720 = 51840\]

So:

\[a \times b = \text{HCF} \times \text{LCM}\]

18. Important CBSE Problem Type: Find HCF and LCM from Product

Example

If:

\[a \times b = 1800\]

and:

\[\text{HCF}(a,b)=12\]

find LCM.

Using:

\[a \times b = \text{HCF} \times \text{LCM}\]
\[1800 = 12 \times \text{LCM}\]
\[\text{LCM} = \frac{1800}{12}\]
\[\text{LCM}=150\]

19. Important CBSE Application: Word Problems on HCF

Problem

Two ropes are 96 cm and 120 cm long. They are to be cut into equal pieces of maximum length. Find the length of each piece.

Solution

Maximum equal length means HCF.

Find:

\[\text{HCF}(96,120)\]

Prime factorisation:

\[96 = 2^5 \times 3\]
\[120 = 2^3 \times 3 \times 5\]

Common lowest powers:

\[2^3 \times 3 = 8 \times 3 = 24\]

Therefore, maximum length of each piece is:

\[24 \text{ cm}\]
CBSE Class 10 • Real Numbers

20. Important CBSE Application: Word Problems on LCM

Problem

Two bells ring at intervals of 12 minutes and 18 minutes. If they ring together now, after how many minutes will they ring together again?

Solution

Together again means LCM.

Find:

\[\text{LCM}(12,18)\]
\[12 = 2^2 \times 3\]
\[18 = 2 \times 3^2\]

LCM:

\[2^2 \times 3^2 = 4 \times 9 = 36\]

Therefore, they will ring together again after:

\[36 \text{ minutes}\]

21. Decimal Expansion of Rational Numbers

Let:

\[\frac{p}{q}\]

be a rational number in lowest form, where \(q \neq 0\).

The decimal expansion depends on the prime factors of \(q\).

Case 1: Terminating Decimal

If \(q\) has only prime factors 2 and 5, then the decimal expansion terminates.

That means:

\[q = 2^m5^n\]

Example:

\[\frac{7}{40}\]

Here:

\[40 = 2^3 \times 5\]

Only 2 and 5 are present.

Therefore:

\[\frac{7}{40}\]

has terminating decimal expansion.

\[\frac{7}{40}=0.175\]

Case 2: Non-Terminating Recurring Decimal

If \(q\) has any prime factor other than 2 and 5, then the decimal expansion is non-terminating recurring.

Example:

\[\frac{5}{12}\]

Here:

\[12 = 2^2 \times 3\]

Since 3 is present, decimal expansion is non-terminating recurring.

\[\frac{5}{12}=0.41666\dots\]

23. Proof of Irrationality

Class 10 CBSE frequently asks proof-based questions like:

\[\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5}\]

are irrational.

Important Lemma

If \(p\) is a prime number and:

\[p \mid a^2\]

then:

\[p \mid a\]

Example:

If:

\[2 \mid a^2\]

then:

\[2 \mid a\]

This result is very important in irrationality proofs.

24. Prove That \(\sqrt{2}\) Is Irrational

Assume that \(\sqrt{2}\) is rational.

Then:

\[\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}\]

where \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime integers and \(q \neq 0\).

Squaring both sides:

\[2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2}\]
\[p^2 = 2q^2\]

So:

\[2 \mid p^2\]

Therefore:

\[2 \mid p\]

Let:

\[p = 2k\]

Now:

\[p^2 = 4k^2\]

Substitute:

\[4k^2 = 2q^2\]
\[q^2 = 2k^2\]

So:

\[2 \mid q^2\]

Therefore:

\[2 \mid q\]

So both \(p\) and \(q\) are divisible by 2.

This contradicts the fact that \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime.

Therefore:

\[\sqrt{2}\]

is irrational.

25. Prove That \(\sqrt{3}\) Is Irrational

Assume that \(\sqrt{3}\) is rational.

Then:

\[\sqrt{3} = \frac{p}{q}\]

where \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime integers and \(q \neq 0\).

Squaring:

\[3 = \frac{p^2}{q^2}\]
\[p^2 = 3q^2\]

So:

\[3 \mid p^2\]

Therefore:

\[3 \mid p\]

Let:

\[p = 3k\]

Then:

\[p^2 = 9k^2\]

Substitute:

\[9k^2 = 3q^2\]
\[q^2 = 3k^2\]

So:

\[3 \mid q^2\]

Therefore:

\[3 \mid q\]

So both \(p\) and \(q\) are divisible by 3.

This contradicts the assumption that \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime.

Therefore:

\[\sqrt{3}\]

is irrational.

26. Prove That \(5+\sqrt{2}\) Is Irrational

Assume:

\[5+\sqrt{2}\]

is rational.

Let:

\[5+\sqrt{2}=r\]

where \(r\) is rational.

Then:

\[\sqrt{2}=r-5\]

Since \(r\) is rational and 5 is rational:

\[r-5\]

is rational.

So:

\[\sqrt{2}\]

is rational.

But this is false because \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.

Therefore:

\[5+\sqrt{2}\]

is irrational.

27. Prove That \(3\sqrt{2}\) Is Irrational

Assume:

\[3\sqrt{2}\]

is rational.

Let:

\[3\sqrt{2}=r\]

where \(r\) is rational.

Then:

\[\sqrt{2} = \frac{r}{3}\]

Since \(r\) is rational and 3 is rational:

\[\frac{r}{3}\]

is rational.

So:

\[\sqrt{2}\]

is rational.

But \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.

This is a contradiction.

Therefore:

\[3\sqrt{2}\]

is irrational.

\[a-b \mid a^2-b^2\]

\[a^2-b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)\]

\[a-b \mid a^n-b^n\]

for every positive integer \(n\).

\[a+b \mid a^n+b^n\]

when \(n\) is odd.

29. Topper Proof: \(n^2-n\) Is Divisible by 2

Prove:

\[n^2-n\]

is divisible by 2 for every integer \(n\).

Solution:

\[n^2-n = n(n-1)\]

Here \(n-1\) and \(n\) are consecutive integers.

Among two consecutive integers, one is even.

Therefore:

\[2 \mid n(n-1)\]

Hence:

\[2 \mid n^2-n\]
CBSE Class 10 • Real Numbers

30. Topper Proof: \(n^3-n\) Is Divisible by 6

Prove:

\[n^3-n\]

is divisible by 6 for every integer \(n\).

Solution:

\[n^3-n = n(n^2-1)\]
\[n^3-n = n(n-1)(n+1)\]

These are three consecutive integers:

\[n-1,\ n,\ n+1\]

Among three consecutive integers:

  • one is divisible by 2
  • one is divisible by 3

Therefore, the product is divisible by:

\[2 \times 3 = 6\]

Hence:

\[6 \mid n^3-n\]

31. Topper Proof: \(n^5-n\) Is Divisible by 30

Prove:

\[30 \mid n^5-n\]

for every integer \(n\).

Since:

\[30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5\]

we prove divisibility by 2, 3, and 5.

Divisibility by 2

If \(n\) is even, then \(n^5-n\) is even.

If \(n\) is odd, then \(n^5\) is odd.

So:

\[n^5-n = \text{odd} - \text{odd} = \text{even}\]

Therefore:

\[2 \mid n^5-n\]

Divisibility by 3

Every integer leaves remainder 0, 1, or 2 when divided by 3.

If:

\[n \equiv 0 \pmod 3\]

then:

\[n^5-n \equiv 0^5-0 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\]

If:

\[n \equiv 1 \pmod 3\]

then:

\[n^5-n \equiv 1^5-1 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\]

If:

\[n \equiv 2 \pmod 3\]

then:

\[n^5-n \equiv 2^5-2 = 32-2=30\]
\[30 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\]

So:

\[3 \mid n^5-n\]

Divisibility by 5

Every integer leaves remainder 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 when divided by 5.

Check:

\[0^5-0=0\]
\[1^5-1=0\]
\[2^5-2=32-2=30\]
\[3^5-3=243-3=240\]
\[4^5-4=1024-4=1020\]

Each is divisible by 5.

So:

\[5 \mid n^5-n\]

Since 2, 3, and 5 are pairwise coprime:

\[30 \mid n^5-n\]

32. Modular Arithmetic for CBSE Topper Level

Modular arithmetic means working with remainders.

\[a \equiv b \pmod m\]

means:

\[m \mid (a-b)\]

Example:

\[17 \equiv 2 \pmod 5\]

because:

\[17-2=15\]

and:

\[5 \mid 15\]

Divisibility in Modular Form

\[a \mid b\]

means:

\[b \equiv 0 \pmod a\]

Example:

\[7 \mid 56\]

means:

\[56 \equiv 0 \pmod 7\]

33. Remainder Example for Toppers

Find the remainder when:

\[3^{100}\]

is divided by 8.

We know:

\[3^2 = 9\]
\[9 \equiv 1 \pmod 8\]

Now:

\[3^{100} = (3^2)^{50}\]
\[3^{100} \equiv 1^{50} \pmod 8\]
\[3^{100} \equiv 1 \pmod 8\]

Therefore, the remainder is:

\[1\]
CBSE Class 10 • Real Numbers

34. Another Remainder Example

Find the remainder when:

\[2^{50}\]

is divided by 7.

Observe powers of 2 modulo 7:

\[2^1 = 2\]
\[2^2 = 4\]
\[2^3 = 8 \equiv 1 \pmod 7\]

So the cycle length is 3.

Now:

\[50 = 3 \times 16 + 2\]

Therefore:

\[2^{50} = (2^3)^{16} \times 2^2\]
\[2^{50} \equiv 1^{16} \times 4 \pmod 7\]
\[2^{50} \equiv 4 \pmod 7\]

So the remainder is:

\[4\]

35. CBSE Exam Strategy: How Average Students Become Toppers

Step 1: Master Definitions

Learn clearly:

\[a \mid b \iff b = ak,\quad k \in \mathbb{Z}\]
\[a = bq + r,\quad 0 \leq r < b\]
\[\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM} = \text{Product of two numbers}\]

Step 2: Master Euclid’s Algorithm

Practice at least 20 examples.

Important style:

\[a = bq + r\]

Continue until remainder becomes 0.

Final non-zero divisor is HCF.

Step 3: Master Prime Factorisation

Practice:

  • finding prime factors
  • HCF using lowest powers
  • LCM using highest powers
  • decimal expansion using denominator factors

Step 4: Master Proof Writing

CBSE toppers write proof in clean steps:

  1. Assume the opposite
  2. Write in fraction form
  3. Use squaring
  4. Use prime divisibility
  5. Show contradiction
  6. Conclude irrationality

Step 5: Master Word Problems

HCF means:

  • maximum size
  • largest length
  • greatest number of equal groups

LCM means:

  • together again
  • minimum time
  • repeated events
  • smallest common number

Mistake Correction
Thinking \(a \mid b\) means \(b \mid a\) Not always true

Confusing HCF and LCM in word problems Maximum equal division = HCF; together again = LCM

39. Final Topper Formula Sheet

\[a \mid b \iff b = ak,\quad k \in \mathbb{Z}\]
\[a = bq + r,\quad 0 \leq r < b\]
\[\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r)\]
\[\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM} = a \times b\]
\[q = 2^m5^n \Rightarrow \text{terminating decimal}\]
\[q \neq 2^m5^n \Rightarrow \text{non-terminating recurring decimal}\]
\[a-b \mid a^n-b^n\]
\[a+b \mid a^n+b^n \quad \text{if } n \text{ is odd}\]
\[n^3-n = n(n-1)(n+1)\]
\[6 \mid n^3-n\]
\[30 \mid n^5-n\]
